    What happens if an electric car has a bump

    Accident damage on an EV can be repaired just as well as on any other car, but high voltage calls for its own precautions. Here’s what to do and what the garage checks.

    17 March 2026Electric & hybrid

    An electric car has bodywork, bumpers and headlights like any other, and most everyday knocks are repaired the same way. The difference lies underneath: a large battery, high-voltage cables and safety systems designed to disconnect it in a crash. That changes a few things, both at the scene and in the workshop.

    Right after the impact

    • If anyone is hurt or there is any danger, call 112 and say it is an electric vehicle.
    • If the car shows a high-voltage system warning, switch it off as the handbook describes and don’t try to keep driving.
    • Don’t touch orange cables or components: orange marks high voltage.
    • If you notice smoke, a strange smell, crackling or unusual heat underneath, move away from the car and call the emergency services.
    • Ask roadside assistance for a suitable recovery truck. Many EVs must not be towed with the driven wheels on the ground; the handbook will say.

    If the bump is minor, the car shows no warnings and drives normally, you can usually take it to the garage yourself. It’s still worth having it checked.

    Hits from underneath are the trickiest

    The high-voltage battery usually sits in the floor of the car, protected by a casing. A high kerb, a rock on a dirt track or a speed bump taken too quickly can strike it without leaving a mark on the bodywork. If you’ve felt a hard knock from below, even if the car still works, ask for the casing and mountings to be checked. A deformation can affect the battery’s sealing or cooling over time.

    What a properly equipped garage does

    Before starting bodywork on an EV, trained staff follow the manufacturer’s procedure to de-energise the high-voltage system and confirm that it is safe. Then they:

    1. Run a full diagnostic check to see whether the impact has logged fault codes in the battery, electronics or driver-assistance systems.
    2. Check the structure on a chassis jig after a significant impact, because the rigidity of the body also protects the battery.
    3. Repair or replace the affected parts to the manufacturer’s specifications.
    4. Control the temperature during painting: some manufacturers limit the heat the battery may be exposed to while the paint cures.
    5. Recalibrate sensors and cameras if bumpers, the windscreen or brackets have been disturbed.

    Insurance and the battery

    The process with the insurer is the same as for any car: report, assessment and repair. What may change is the scope. If battery damage is suspected, the assessment may require specific tests and, in some cases, a manufacturer diagnosis. Ask for everything to be recorded in the report and the estimate.

    Each manufacturer has its own procedures for repairing electric cars. If you are told something can’t be repaired or needs special work, ask why: you are entitled to understand it.

    Not more dangerous, just different

    Electric cars pass the same crash tests as other cars and their batteries are well protected. There’s no reason to be afraid, but there is every reason to have it repaired by someone who knows the system. A poorly repaired impact near the battery can cause problems long afterwards.
